## Why This Research Matters

This paper marked the beginning of HumemAI’s research direction on explicit memory architectures for AI. The work was shaped in part by the cognitive-science research of [Endel Tulving](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=OxmLLMEAAAAJ&hl=en), who argued that **episodic memory** is central to conscious experience. Alongside **semantic memory**, it forms one of the two core pillars of explicit memory. That framing helped establish the research question behind this paper: whether explicit memory structures are also important for human-like cognition in AI systems.

Human memory enables mental time travel: reconstructing the past, interpreting the present, and anticipating the future. This paper explored whether some of that capability can be modeled computationally through **human-like memory systems** in AI.

## Knowledge Graphs as AI Memory

To model AI memory, this work evaluated several ways to represent stored experience and ultimately chose [**knowledge graphs**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02320), which are readable by both humans and machines. From there, the question became whether an explicit memory system would materially improve the behavior of an AI agent.

To validate the idea, HumemAI built a small toy environment called [RoomEnv-v0](https://github.com/humemai/room-env) and tested handcrafted memory-management functions inside it. The result was a clear early signal that structured memory could be useful for AI agents.

## Abstract of the Paper

> Inspired by cognitive science theory, we explicitly model an agent with both **semantic** and **episodic** memory systems, demonstrating that a combination of both outperforms a single-memory approach. To validate our approach, we designed and released our own challenging environment, **"The Room"**, compatible with OpenAI Gym. In this environment, an agent must learn to encode, store, and retrieve memories effectively to maximize rewards. The Room environment enables a hybrid intelligence setup where machines and humans can collaborate. Our results show that two agents collaborating with each other achieve better performance than a single agent acting alone.
